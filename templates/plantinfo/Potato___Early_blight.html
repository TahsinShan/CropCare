🥔 Potato Early Blight

📖 Overview

Early blight, caused by the fungus Alternaria solani, is a common and destructive foliar disease of potatoes. It thrives in warm, wet conditions and leads to dark leaf spots, defoliation, and reduced tuber yield and quality. Early blight can also affect stems and tubers, causing lesions and rot.

🧬 Causes & Pathogen Biology

Alternaria solani survives in infected plant debris and soil. It produces spores called conidia that spread via wind, rain splash, and irrigation water. Infection occurs when spores land on moist leaf surfaces, germinate, and penetrate through stomata or wounds.

Optimal Spread Conditions:

  • Temperature: 24–29°C (75–85°F)
  • Relative Humidity: ≥ 85%
  • Leaf wetness duration: ≥ 6 hours
  • Periods of frequent rain or overhead irrigation

🌡 Symptoms & Diagnostic Clues

  • Dark brown to black, irregularly shaped leaf spots with concentric rings ("target" appearance)
  • Lesions often start on older, lower leaves and progress upward
  • Yellowing and premature leaf drop around infected spots
  • Stem lesions that are dark and sunken
  • Tuber infections causing rough, dark, sunken areas (black scurf)
  • Severe infections cause defoliation and reduced tuber yield

📊 Disease Progression Stages

Stage 1: Initial Infection

Small, dark brown spots with faint concentric rings appear on older lower leaves. No stem or tuber symptoms yet.

Stage 2: Moderate

Lesions enlarge and coalesce, yellowing around spots increases. Some dark lesions visible on stems. Early signs of tuber infection may appear.

Stage 3: Severe

Extensive leaf necrosis and defoliation. Stem lesions widespread. Tubers show dark sunken lesions and rot. Yield and tuber quality severely affected.

🛠 Treatments & Control Options

  • Apply fungicides such as chlorothalonil, mancozeb, or strobilurins preventively or at early symptom onset
  • Rotate fungicides to reduce risk of resistance development
  • Remove and destroy infected plant debris to reduce inoculum sources
  • Ensure good field sanitation and crop rotation with non-host crops
  • Improve air circulation by proper plant spacing and avoid overhead irrigation

🌿 Cultural Prevention Techniques

  • Practice crop rotation with non-host crops for at least 2–3 years
  • Use certified disease-free seed potatoes
  • Hill soil around plants to protect tubers from sunlight and disease
  • Maintain balanced fertilization to promote healthy plants
  • Regularly remove volunteer potato plants and weeds that may harbor the pathogen

📅 Early Detection & Monitoring

Scout lower leaves weekly during warm, humid weather. Look for characteristic target-shaped leaf spots. Monitor weather conditions to anticipate periods favorable for disease development and time fungicide applications accordingly.
